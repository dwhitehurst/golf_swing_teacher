P1 — Address

FACE-ON
  • Favor the lead side slightly so the setup already resembles where the body is headed at impact.
  • Let the shaft and lead arm form a simple, structured relationship rather than adding extra hand manipulation.
  • Use a neutral athletic posture with the trail leg stable and slightly braced.
  • Keep feet, hips, and shoulders organized so the motion can begin from a balanced, repeatable base.
  Why it matters: The closer the setup is to a useful impact pattern, the fewer last-second compensations are needed later in the swing.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • Set posture and spacing so the body can turn without standing up or reaching for the ball.
  • Keep the knees softly flexed and pressure balanced through the middle/front of the feet.
  • Let the arms hang naturally from the shoulders instead of pushing them away from the body.
  • Leave comfortable space between the grip and thighs, with the body aligned in a neutral starting position.
  Why it matters: A clean down-the-line setup makes the rest of the swing easier to repeat because balance, posture, and spacing are already organized.


P2 — Early Backswing

FACE-ON
  • When the club reaches parallel to the ground, it should remain in a structured relationship to the trail side rather than getting sucked far behind you.
  • Let the lead shoulder work down and across as the torso turns instead of making a flat, level shoulder turn.
  • Allow the hands and wrists to begin setting naturally rather than forcing an early hinge.
  • Keep the arms and body moving together so the takeaway feels connected.
  Why it matters: A connected early motion gives the backswing a simple shape and reduces the need to reroute the club later.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • Let the clubface remain organized rather than rolling dramatically open during the takeaway.
  • Keep the shaft centered with the hands instead of dragging it excessively inside or pushing it outside.
  • Allow the club to move back on plane as a result of the body turn rather than hand manipulation.
  • Blend body turn, arm movement, and wrist set into one continuous takeaway.
  Why it matters: P2 strongly influences the plane, wrist alignments, and delivery pattern that follow.


P3 — Lead Arm Parallel — Backswing

FACE-ON
  • The lead arm reaches roughly parallel to the ground while the club has hinged into a more vertical relationship.
  • Keep the trail leg supportive and stable rather than swaying away from the target.
  • Continue turning the shoulders more than the hips so the backswing develops coil without losing balance.
  • Keep the arms organized with the torso rather than lifting them independently.
  Why it matters: This is where the backswing begins to look fully organized: body turn, arm structure, and club set are working together.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • The shaft should now look organized on a useful plane rather than dramatically across or behind the body.
  • Let the lead shoulder work down while the trail shoulder works up as the torso turns.
  • Keep the trail elbow reasonably connected to the side of the torso.
  • Continue coiling the upper body while the lower body remains stable enough to support the turn.
  Why it matters: A well-organized P3 makes reaching the top simpler and makes the eventual change of direction less complicated.


P4 — Top of Swing

FACE-ON
  • Keep the trail leg braced enough to support the turn rather than allowing a large sway.
  • Let the head remain relatively centered as the shoulders complete their turn.
  • The lead shoulder works under the chin while the trail shoulder moves behind you.
  • Complete the upper-body turn without losing posture or balance.
  Why it matters: A strong top position combines coil, structure, and control so the downswing can begin without a rescue move.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • The shoulders should turn and tilt on a useful plane while the body keeps its depth.
  • Keep the clubface and wrists organized rather than dramatically cupped, bowed, open, or shut.
  • Maintain some connection between the upper trail arm and the torso.
  • Arrive at the top poised and balanced instead of stretched or collapsed.
  Why it matters: The top is less about hitting a frozen pose and more about arriving organized enough to change direction cleanly.


P5 — Lead Arm Parallel — Downswing

FACE-ON
  • As the lead arm returns toward parallel, keep width and structure while the club is being delivered toward the ball.
  • Let pressure begin moving toward the lead side early rather than waiting until impact.
  • The lower body begins opening while the upper body and arms remain in sequence.
  • Allow the knees and pelvis to start re-centering toward the target without losing posture.
  Why it matters: This transition checkpoint largely determines whether the club will approach impact with useful rotation, pressure shift, and compression.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • Let the club shallow as a consequence of sequencing rather than deliberately throwing the hands behind you.
  • Keep the body angles stable while the lower body begins rotating toward the target.
  • Pressure should already be moving into the lead side.
  • The knees begin re-aligning toward the target while the club approaches from a more deliverable position.
  Why it matters: A good P5 blends shallowing, body rotation, and pressure shift instead of treating them as separate moves.


P6 — Delivery Position

FACE-ON
  • The hands and club work underneath the chin as the body continues shifting and rotating toward the target.
  • The trail leg is moving inward and under the body rather than remaining stuck behind you.
  • Keep wrist structure long enough for the club to arrive with useful compression instead of releasing early.
  • Pressure is clearly moving into the lead side while posture remains athletic.
  Why it matters: P6 is a delivery checkpoint: the body, hands, wrists, and club should look organized for impact rather than preparing for a last-second correction.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • Keep the clubface organized as the club approaches the ball; avoid arriving dramatically open and needing a flip.
  • The shaft stays roughly level with or slightly behind the hands as it approaches impact.
  • The lead leg begins to become visible while the trail knee works toward the lead knee.
  • The shoulders remain sequenced with the lower body and pressure continues shifting left.
  Why it matters: A well-organized P6 allows the body to keep rotating through impact instead of stalling so the hands can save the shot.


P7 — Impact

FACE-ON
  • Pressure and body mass are clearly forward into the lead side.
  • The hands are ahead of the ball, producing forward shaft lean with an iron.
  • The trail leg has moved inward and under the body while the lead side supports the strike.
  • Chest, hips, and pressure all favor the lead side rather than hanging back.
  Why it matters: Impact is the result of everything before it. A useful impact pattern makes solid contact and compression much easier.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • The hips are more open than the shoulders, showing lower-body sequencing through the strike.
  • The lead side is visible and supporting the body while the trail side continues moving through.
  • The trail elbow stays close enough to the body to support a shallow, organized delivery.
  • Pressure is predominantly in the lead foot and the hands remain ahead of the clubhead.
  Why it matters: At impact, rotation, pressure shift, and arm structure come together. The goal is a body that can keep moving rather than stopping at the ball.


P8 — Post Impact — Club Parallel

FACE-ON
  • Keep turning and tilting through the strike instead of standing up immediately after impact.
  • The lead shoulder works up and away as the body continues rotating.
  • The knees move closer together as the lower body rebalances.
  • Allow the hands and wrists to rotate and release naturally rather than consciously flipping the club.
  Why it matters: A good release is driven by continuing body motion. The club is being carried through by rotation, extension, and momentum.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • Continue shoulder turn and tilt through the hitting area while maintaining posture.
  • The trail knee keeps moving toward the lead knee as the body rotates.
  • The club releases naturally as the arms extend away from the body.
  • Keep rotation and side bend working together instead of lifting or spinning out.
  Why it matters: P8 shows whether the strike flowed into a balanced release or whether the motion stalled at the ball.


P9 — Follow Through

FACE-ON
  • The trail leg continues moving in and under the body as pressure remains strongly on the lead side.
  • The arms extend away from the body, creating width through the release.
  • The lead shoulder stays high while the torso continues turning.
  • Maintain balance as the knees move closer and the body continues into the finish.
  Why it matters: The quality of P9 reflects the quality of the motion through impact. Efficient swings keep moving rather than stopping after contact.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • Keep the shoulders rotating and tilting as the torso moves toward the finish.
  • The trail knee works inside toward the lead leg as the body rebalances.
  • The lead side continues working upward while the arms extend outward.
  • Turn and tilt continue together instead of the body simply standing straight up.
  Why it matters: A continuous follow-through is a sign that speed, balance, and rotation were allowed to continue beyond the ball.


P10 — Finish

FACE-ON
  • Finish tall enough to be balanced, but without losing all of the body’s athletic structure.
  • The lower body has fully rotated through and the belt buckle finishes slightly ahead of the chest.
  • The knees come together and the trail foot finishes up on the toe.
  • Settle into a balanced finish with the club resting comfortably around the back of the shoulders.
  Why it matters: A complete, balanced finish is evidence that the body shifted, rotated, and released through the ball rather than fighting for balance after impact.

DOWN-THE-LINE
  • Maintain some side bend and neck tilt through the finish rather than snapping completely upright.
  • The knees and thighs come together as the body completes its rebalance.
  • Pressure finishes on the lead side while the trail foot is fully up on the toe.
  • The club wraps around the neck or back of the head in a relaxed, fully completed finish.
  Why it matters: The finish is the final checkpoint: efficient swings tend to end in balance because the motion leading into the finish was connected.
